By the time you reach the sandbag lunges, you've already done six stations and seven runs. Your quads are tired, your grip is tired, and the wall balls are still waiting. The 100 meters of lunges aren't usually where people lose the most time, but they're where many people lose their legs for the finish.

The goal at this station is simple: get through cleanly, at an even pace, with as much left in your quads as possible.

Know the standard before you lunge

The back knee has to touch the ground on each rep, and you need to stand fully between lunges. No-reps are the hidden time cost here. A judge asking you to repeat reps adds seconds and extra fatigue you didn't plan for. Practice touching the knee lightly every time, so it becomes automatic.

Weights depend on division. In the standard format, Open men usually carry 20 kg and Open women 10 kg, while Pro men carry 30 kg and Pro women 20 kg. Check the current rulebook for your event.

Carry it high and stay tall

Most racers place the bag across the back of the shoulders, like a back squat. It keeps your chest up and lets your hips move freely. Hug the bag with your hands wide and your elbows slightly down. If the bag starts sliding, stop for one second and reset it. Fighting a slipping bag for 20 meters costs far more.

Keep your torso upright and your front shin roughly vertical. Leaning forward shifts the load onto your lower back and front knee, and it makes standing up much harder.

Pick a stride and a tempo

A comfortable stride length, roughly a normal walking step and a half, covers the ground well without stressing the hips. Then pick a tempo you could hold for the whole 100 meters, such as one lunge every two to three seconds.

The most common mistake is racing the first 30 meters. If you go out too fast, you'll be taking long breaks by the halfway mark. Even pacing almost always wins.

Break it before it breaks you

Plan short standing pauses in advance. For many age-group racers, a three-breath pause every 20 or 25 lunges works well. Keep the bag on your shoulders during the pause. Putting it down and picking it back up wastes time and energy.

Train the transition to wall balls

Lunges and wall balls both hammer the quads, with only the final run between them. Practice that exact sequence in training: 50 to 100 meters of lunges, a 1km run, then a set of 30 to 50 wall balls. Learn how your legs feel, and practice relaxing your stride on that last run so your quads can recover.

A simple progression. Start with 2 rounds of 50 meters of lunges with a moderate load, twice a week. Over four to six weeks, build to a full 100 meters at race weight, followed by the run and wall balls once a week.

Recovery after lunge-heavy sessions

Expect sore quads and glutes for a day or two after big lunge sessions. Easy cycling, light walking, and gentle mobility help more than total rest. Space your heaviest lunge day at least 48 hours from your hardest running session.
